🧭 The arc — why binary, why now

Nine ideas that turn a voltage across a wire into a transformer running a conversation.

🧒 Imagine this…

You have a tiny light switch. Flip up = 1 (yes), flip down = 0 (no). That's it. That's the whole secret of computers.

Now imagine 208 billion of these tiny switches, all packed onto a chip the size of a postage stamp, all flipping on and off billions of times every second in a perfect dance. If you teach them a pattern like "when these 3 switches are on, turn that other switch on", you've made a rule. Stack enough rules together and you can add numbers, draw pictures, play music, and even have a conversation with a computer.

This section tells you the 9 big ideas that get us from one tiny switch all the way up to AI.

1. Switches

A transistor is a voltage-controlled switch. On = 1, off = 0. Digital computing exists because silicon gates have sharp noise margins — a wide, well-defined gap between "definitely 0" and "definitely 1", with the in-between zone forbidden. That clean snap is why a signal can pass through a billion stages without drifting into garbage.

2. Gates

Combining transistors gives AND / OR / NAND. Every arithmetic operation, every ML layer, is a tree of NAND gates deep down.

3. Numbers

Positional binary (2ⁿ weights) lets n bits encode 2ⁿ values. Addition is carry propagation between gates.

4. Signed Ints

Two's complement is the unique encoding where one adder circuit handles both signed and unsigned arithmetic correctly.

5. Floats

IEEE 754 gives us scientific notation in hardware — sign · 2ᵉˣᵖ · mantissa — trading precision for range.

6. ISA

Assembly is the contract between compiler and silicon. x86/ARM/RISC-V all expose integer + float + SIMD instructions.

7. Compilers

LLVM/GCC turn high-level code into instruction streams tuned for the CPU's pipeline, cache, and vector units.

8. Parallel math

GPUs evolved from graphics (many floats, same op) to general math engines. CUDA / ROCm expose this as programmable parallel arithmetic.

9. AI Precision

Training shifted from FP32 → FP16/BF16 → FP8 → FP4. Lower precision = more throughput, less energy, same model quality (if done carefully).

⚛️ Physics — bits are real things

A bit isn't abstract. It's a charge on a capacitor, a voltage at a node, or a magnetic domain on disk.

🧒 Imagine this…

When grown-ups say "there's a 1 inside the computer", it sounds like magic. But it's actually a real, physical thing — like a marble you could touch, if you were tiny enough.

A "1" is usually a little puddle of electricity sitting in a tiny cup on the chip. A "0" is an empty cup. That's it! Some chips use: a cup of electricity (memory, called DRAM). Some use: a spinning tiny magnet pointing north or south (hard drive). Some use: a locked-in electron that refuses to leak out (your phone's storage, called Flash).

Every single "thing" in a computer — your photos, your game, this page — is a huge pile of these tiny puddles, arranged in just the right pattern. Bits are real. You could, in principle, count them with a microscope.

MOSFET — the switch

A gate voltage controls whether electrons can flow from source to drain. Modern 3 nm transistors are ~20 atoms wide. Below that, quantum tunneling makes them leak.

VDD ≈ 0.7 V · VTH ≈ 0.3 V · switching energy ≈ ½·C·V² ≈ 10⁻¹⁸ J

Noise margin — why binary wins

Three or more voltage levels (ternary) is thermodynamically worse: every extra level halves the noise budget. Binary is the sweet spot between bandwidth and error robustness.

Landauer limit

Erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 ≈ 2.85 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K. We're about 10⁶× above it today — room to go.

Clock & propagation

At 5 GHz a signal has 200 ps to cross a chip. Light travels 6 cm in that time. This is why chips are small and pipelines deep.

Memory cells

DRAM: 1 bit = 1 transistor + 1 capacitor (must refresh every ~64 ms).
SRAM: 1 bit = 6 transistors (stable, fast, expensive → caches).
Flash: trapped electrons in a floating gate (slow write, non-volatile).

Heat — the real limit

Dennard scaling broke ~2006: shrinking transistors no longer reduces power density. That's why clocks stopped climbing past ~5 GHz and multi-core + parallelism took over.

What "3 nm" actually is 20 atoms wide

A silicon atom is ~0.22 nm across. So "3 nm" ≈ 13 silicon atoms lined up. The oxide gate dielectric inside is only ~1 nm = 4-5 atoms thick.

But — and this is key — "3 nm" is a marketing name, not a real feature size. The actual transistor gate is ~16-20 nm; "3 nm" refers to density class. See §Scale "Sidebar · What is 4 nm process?" for details.

Why this matters quantum floor

Below ~1 nm, quantum tunneling (see card 1 below) makes transistors leak no matter what. We're already within 5× of the atomic limit. That's why Moore's Law is slowing — not engineering laziness, physics.

The reason nobody builds chips at 0.5 nm isn't money. It's that the universe doesn't allow it with silicon.

"8 atoms" vs "3 atomic layers" two different rulers

You'll see both phrasings used for the same oxide thickness — they are not the same count.

"Atoms wide" — counts atomic diameters laid end-to-end, as if packing marbles.
Silicon atom ≈ 0.22 nm across.
So 2 nm oxide ≈ 2 / 0.22 ≈ 9 atoms wide.

"Atomic layers" — counts crystal repeat units stacked like pancakes. Each layer = one atom plus the bond gap to the next layer.
Si-Si layer spacing ≈ 0.27 nm. SiO₂ layer spacing ≈ 0.35 nm.
So 1 nm oxide ≈ 1 / 0.35 ≈ 3 atomic layers.

Rule of thumb: "N atomic layers" ≈ 1.3× to 1.6× as much thickness as "N atoms wide," because layers include bond spacing. When chip-makers say "the gate oxide is 3 atomic layers," they mean ~1 nm of dielectric — the physical limit before tunneling ruins everything.

Å — the ångström atom-sized unit

1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1 nm = 100 pm. Named after Swedish physicist Anders Jonas Ångström (1814-1874), founder of spectroscopy. The letter is Swedish: "a with a ring on top".

Why it exists: it's the natural ruler of atoms.

  • Hydrogen atom ≈ 1 Å
  • Silicon atom ≈ 2.2 Å
  • Si-Si bond ≈ 2.35 Å
  • DNA double helix ≈ 20 Å
  • Visible light wavelength ≈ 4,000-7,000 Å
  • X-rays ≈ 0.1-100 Å

Not SI: formally deprecated in 1960 in favour of nm and pm. But physicists, crystallographers, and spectroscopists still use it everywhere because one typical atom = one unit. Clean.

In this dashboard: the Si atom is shown at "100 pm" on the size ladder — that's 1 Å. When chip-makers say "1 nm gate oxide = 3 atomic layers," that's literally 10 Å = 3 layers of ~3.3 Å.

Fun: the only physics unit whose symbol carries a diacritic. Even Unicode had two codepoints for it (U+212B ANGSTROM SIGN vs U+00C5 Å) before standardizing.

Deep dives on the four big ones

0 · MOSFET — the switch itself the atom of computing

MOSFET = Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor. It's a tiny voltage-controlled valve for electric current — the single invention (1959, Atalla & Kahng at Bell Labs) that all digital computing is built on. A modern Blackwell GPU = 208 billion of them.

3 terminals:

  • Source — where electrons come from
  • Drain — where electrons go to
  • Gate — the control knob (doesn't touch the channel — separated by a thin oxide insulator)
Gate (control) │ ┌──┴──┐ ← thin oxide (insulator, ~1 nm) Source ═════════ Drain ▲▲▲▲▲▲▲▲ ← channel (opens when gate is on) silicon body

How it switches: apply +voltage to the gate → electric field pulls electrons into the channel → current flows source→drain (ON = 1). Remove the voltage → channel empties, current stops (OFF = 0).

Why "field-effect": the gate doesn't inject any current — it just creates an electric field that herds electrons below. So the gate uses almost zero power (just capacitor charging). This is the trick that makes billions-of-transistors chips possible.

Garden-hose analogy: source → drain is the hose. The gate is your hand squeezing the hose. Squeeze harder (more voltage) → more flow. Release → flow stops. Your hand doesn't touch the water.

Two flavours, always paired (CMOS):

  • NMOS — turns on with +gate voltage (electrons carry current)
  • PMOS — turns on with −gate voltage (holes carry current)
  • One NMOS + one PMOS = CMOS inverter, the base cell of every digital chip

Modern variants: planar MOSFET → FinFET (2011, gate wraps 3 sides) → GAAFET / Nanosheet (2022-25, gate wraps all 4 sides). Each change was to fight leakage as gates shrank — see cards 1 and 4 for why.

1 · Quantum tunneling the ~20-atom wall

In classical physics, an electron can't cross an insulator unless it has enough energy. In quantum physics, it can simply appear on the other side with some probability. That probability grows exponentially as the insulator gets thinner.

In a MOSFET, the gate oxide separates the gate from the channel. At 2003's 90 nm nodes the oxide was ~2 nm thick — 8 atoms. At 1 nm it's about 3 atomic layers. Below that, electrons tunnel through all the time, even when the transistor should be off.

Intel's 45 nm node (2007) moved from SiO₂ to hafnium-oxide (HfO₂) — a thicker high-k material with the same electrical effect — specifically to fight tunneling.

Why this is a hard limit: you can't just "shield" against quantum tunneling. It's not a defect — it's how the universe works. Once leakage current exceeds switching current, the transistor stops being a switch. That's why Moore's Law is really slowing down, not just getting expensive.

2 · Why ternary is thermodynamically worse 3 levels ≠ 1.5× better

Naïve intuition: if 2 voltage levels (0 V, 1 V) can store 1 bit, then 3 levels (0 V, 0.5 V, 1 V) should store log₂(3) ≈ 1.58 bits — free information! But it's a trap.

The catch: you still only have the same 0→1 V voltage range to work with. Adding a middle level halves the gap between adjacent levels — so the "noise budget" (how much a signal can wobble before being misread) drops from 0.5 V to 0.25 V. Four levels → 0.17 V. Eight levels → 0.07 V.

Shannon-Hartley: channel capacity ∝ log₂(1 + SNR). Doubling levels adds 1 bit but needs 4× signal-to-noise ratio → 4× the energy per bit.

Thermodynamic cost: at smaller noise margins, you need more capacitance, higher voltage, or lower temperature to reach the same error rate. Every bit-per-signal you squeeze out costs exponentially more energy.

Binary wins because it's the extreme corner: maximum noise margin per level, simplest circuits (just "is it on?"), and gates that restore signals at every stage. Ternary makes sense only where noise is negligible — inside a clean analog audio rack, not on a chip with a million switching neighbours.

Exceptions that prove the rule: NAND flash uses 2-8 levels per cell (MLC/TLC/QLC) — but accepts much higher error rates and fixes them with heavy ECC. PAM-4 signaling in PCIe 6.0 uses 4 levels — but on a short, shielded, well-characterized channel with equalization.

3 · "200 ps to cross a chip" light-speed budget

1 / 5 GHz = 200 picoseconds per clock cycle. That's the entire time budget for one tick of the clock. Within those 200 ps, signals must travel from flip-flop to flip-flop across combinational logic and arrive stable before the next edge.

The physics: electromagnetic signals in a chip travel at ~½ the speed of light in vacuum (through copper and silicon dioxide dielectric). Roughly 15 cm/ns, or 3 cm in 200 ps.

A modern CPU die is ~20 mm wide. So light itself can barely cross the chip once per cycle. Real signals — slowed by wire resistance, gate delays, buffers — cross only a small region before the next tick.

Rule of thumb: a signal can pass through ~20-30 gate delays in one 200 ps cycle. Everything else must be pipelined.

Consequences this creates:

  • Pipelines — break a computation into tiny stages, each fitting in 200 ps
  • Cache hierarchies — L1 must be tiny (within 1-2 ns reach), L2 larger but slower
  • Clock domains — a big chip can't share one clock edge; it's split into regions
  • Wire delay > gate delay — at modern nodes, moving a signal across the die costs more time than the logic itself
c/2 ≈ 15 cm/ns · wire delay · pipeline stages

4 · Why clocks stopped at ~5 GHz the power wall

Dennard scaling (1974): Robert Dennard showed that as you shrink a transistor by ½, you can also drop voltage by ½ — giving you 4× more transistors at the same power. This is what let clocks rise 386 (16 MHz, 1985) → Pentium 4 (3.8 GHz, 2004) — 240× in 20 years.

It broke around 2005-2006. Below ~90 nm, voltage couldn't drop further because:

  • Threshold voltage floor — below ~0.3 V, transistors don't reliably switch on/off
  • Leakage current explodes — static power (even when idle) became as big as dynamic power
  • Quantum tunneling (see card 1) makes thin oxides leak no matter the voltage

Power scales as P = C·V²·f. Once V stopped falling, every extra GHz of f meant more heat. At 4-5 GHz a ~200 W CPU already needs a big heatsink; pushing to 6-10 GHz would need liquid nitrogen. Not commercially viable.

Intel Pentium 4 "Tejas" was designed for 10 GHz. Cancelled in May 2004 — would have drawn 300+ W. That's the moment the industry pivoted to multicore.

The pivot: instead of faster cores, more cores. Core 2 Duo (2006) → Core i7 quad-core (2008) → EPYC 192-core (2024). Clock frequencies have barely moved in 20 years (3-6 GHz), but performance keeps climbing via parallelism, SIMD, and specialized units (tensor cores, AV1 decoders, neural engines).

Occasional record stunts: overclockers with liquid nitrogen hit 9.1 GHz (AMD FX-8350, 2022), but that's one core at -196 °C drawing extreme power — not useful for servers.

⏱️ Clocks — from CPU ticks to atomic seconds

Every digital system needs a heartbeat. Here's the full stack, from a quartz crystal vibrating next to your CPU up to cesium atoms in Colorado that define what a "second" is.

🧒 Imagine this…

Imagine a classroom full of kids doing synchronized jumping jacks. If they jump at random times, it's chaos. But if there's a teacher clapping a steady clap, clap, clap…, everyone jumps together. That clap is a clock.

Every computer needs a clap too — otherwise its billions of tiny switches would flip at random times and answers would be garbage. Inside your phone a tiny quartz crystal is vibrating 32,768 times per second. Inside your CPU, a circuit multiplies that up to 5 billion times per second. Each vibration is one "tick" — one step forward.

The fanciest clocks of all are made from atoms. They vibrate so steadily that in 30 million years they'd drift by less than a second. GPS satellites use them. Without atomic clocks, Google Maps would be off by kilometres.

1 · Quartz crystals — the universal root

The piezoelectric trick 1880, Curies

Quartz is piezoelectric: squeeze it → voltage appears. Apply voltage → it deforms. A precisely-cut quartz bar has a mechanical resonance — like a tuning fork — and the voltage oscillates at exactly that frequency.

Cut a quartz slab into a tuning-fork shape tuned to 32,768 Hz (2¹⁵), mount it in a vacuum-sealed can, and you have the heartbeat of every wristwatch since 1969 (Seiko Astron).

Why 32,768? It's 2¹⁵ — dividing by 2 fifteen times gives exactly 1 Hz. Perfect for digital logic.

Accuracy vs temperature ±20 ppm

A regular quartz is ±20 parts per million — about 10 seconds drift per week. Temperature-compensated variants (TCXO) hit 0.1 ppm. Oven-controlled (OCXO, kept at constant 70 °C inside the can) hit 0.001 ppm — ~1 s per year.

Almost every chip you own — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, phone modem, USB controller, your CPU — starts life with a small quartz crystal soldered nearby.

2 · PLL — how a 25 MHz crystal becomes a 5 GHz CPU

A PLL (Phase-Locked Loop) multiplies frequency: 25 MHz crystal ──► [phase detector] ──► [loop filter] ──► [VCO 5 GHz] ──► CPU ▲ │ │ │ └──── ÷200 divider ◄───────────────────────────┘ The VCO (voltage-controlled oscillator) runs at 5 GHz but its output is divided by 200 and compared with the 25 MHz reference. If they drift apart, the filter nudges the VCO. Result: VCO is "locked" to 200× the crystal — inheriting its stability.

Every modern CPU has dozens of PLLs — different domains (core, ring bus, memory, PCIe, display) run at different multipliers from the same crystal. Frequency scaling (cpupower, Turbo Boost) just changes the multiplier on the fly.

2b · How one crystal feeds dozens of domains

A desktop has exactly one physical oscillator — typically a 25 MHz or 100 MHz crystal on the motherboard. Its job isn't to match any domain's speed; it's to be the single stable reference every domain derives from. Each PLL inside the SoC multiplies that reference to its own target frequency.

Why one crystal is enough architecture

  • Phase-locking — a PLL's output is forced (by feedback) to stay exactly N× the reference. Any ppm drift in the crystal drifts everything proportionally → domains stay coherent.
  • Cheap & tiny — a PLL is ~0.01 mm². Dozens cost nothing. A second crystal would cost a package pin, a PCB trace, and more than the PLL itself.
  • Scaling = just change N — Turbo Boost, cpupower, DVFS P-states all just write a new multiplier register. Crystal never changes → frequency can shift in microseconds.
  • Isolation — a noisy 5 GHz core can't contaminate the 48 MHz USB clock, because they share only the clean reference, not the VCOs.
  • Jitter control — each PLL has its own loop filter tuned for its domain (memory wants low jitter, USB tolerates more). One crystal, many noise budgets.

Typical multiplier table (25 MHz ref) numbers

ref = 25 MHz crystal Domain ×N Result ────────────── ─── ───────── CPU core base ×200 5.0 GHz P-core turbo ×232 5.8 GHz Ring / L3 ×180 4.5 GHz Memory ctrl ×160 4.0 GHz PCIe 5.0 ref ×128 3.2 GHz Display pixel ×var 533 MHz (4K) USB 2.0 ×variant 48 MHz Audio SPDIF ×variant 24.576 MHz
All derived from one crystal via on-chip PLLs.

Orchestra analogy intuition

One conductor's metronome = crystal. Each instrument = a PLL. Violin plays 4 notes per tick, cello 2, piano ½. If the conductor speeds up, every instrument speeds up proportionally — the orchestra stays in sync.

The conductor doesn't need to know any instrument's tempo; the instruments just count ticks.

Hierarchy in real SoCs deep dive

Modern SoCs rarely run dozens of PLLs straight off the 25 MHz crystal — jitter would be bad at the highest frequencies. Instead they use a tree:

25 MHz crystal │ ▼ Master PLL (3.2 GHz, ultra-low-jitter) ├──▶ core PLL ×1.56 → 5.0 GHz ├──▶ ring PLL ×1.40 → 4.5 GHz ├──▶ mem PLL ×1.25 → 4.0 GHz ├──▶ PCIe PLL ×1.00 → 3.2 GHz └──▶ fixed divs ÷N → USB / I²C / SPI

Each branch's PLL only needs a narrow multiplier range — simpler, lower-noise.

What about servers & multi-socket? edge case

Every socket and every PCIe endpoint still has its own crystal(s) — they don't physically share one. Synchronization happens at a higher protocol layer:

  • PCIe Gen3+ can use SSC (Spread Spectrum Clocking) or "common clock" mode where endpoints lock to the root-complex PLL via the link itself.
  • UPI / Infinity Fabric between sockets uses SerDes training to align clocks on boot.
  • PTP (IEEE 1588) distributes time across a network of servers to <100 ns.

Consequences you can observe proof

  • Overclock the BCLK (reference) by 3% → everything — CPU, RAM, PCIe — overclocks by 3%. USB cameras lose frames. This is why BCLK overclocking is rare now; modern chips lock USB/PCIe to separate dividers.
  • A cheap crystal with ±50 ppm error means a 5 GHz core is actually 5.000 ± 0.00025 GHz. Good enough — software schedulers don't care.
  • Turn on Turbo Boost: the core PLL's multiplier register jumps from ×200 to ×232 in under 10 µs. No crystal involved.
  • cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq is literally reading that multiplier × the reference.

3 · The 200 picosecond budget — revisited

At 5 GHz, each clock cycle is 1/5 × 10⁹ = 200 × 10⁻¹² s = 200 picoseconds. In that time:
  • Light travels ~6 cm in vacuum
  • A signal on a real chip wire travels ~3 cm
  • A typical CMOS gate switches in ~5-10 ps — so ~20-40 gates can fire in series
  • An L1 cache hit takes ~4 cycles (800 ps) — already 4× the single-cycle budget
This is why pipelining, clock-domain partitioning, and wire-aware layout dominate chip design. The speed of light is no longer a nice abstraction — it's a daily engineering constraint.

4 · Atomic clocks — defining the second

Cesium-133 SI definition since 1967

Since 1967, one second = 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the radiation emitted by a cesium-133 atom transitioning between two hyperfine energy levels. No reference to astronomy — the second is an atomic quantity.

A cesium fountain clock (NIST-F2) loses or gains less than 1 second in 300 million years. That's ~10⁻¹⁶ relative accuracy.

Louis Essen built the first practical cesium clock at NPL (UK) in 1955.

Why atoms? quantum universality

Every cesium-133 atom in the universe has identical energy levels — physics says so. Unlike a quartz crystal (shaped by a human, aging over time), an atom is a perfect universal reference. Clocks made on Earth, Mars, or Alpha Centauri would agree — if stationary and at the same gravitational potential.

Optical lattice clocks future of time

Strontium-87 or Ytterbium-171 atoms cooled to μK and held in a laser-trap lattice. Transitions are in the optical band (500 THz), so one "tick" is 10⁵× faster than cesium microwave.

Accuracy: ~10⁻¹⁸ — one second in 30 billion years (older than the universe). Can detect gravitational time dilation from 1 cm height difference. Expected to redefine the SI second around 2030.

GPS depends on this km per ns

GPS satellites carry rubidium + cesium clocks. Your phone calculates position from the time difference between signals from 4+ satellites. A 1 ns timing error = 30 cm position error. A 1 μs error = 300 m.

Relativity matters: satellite clocks tick ~38 μs/day faster than ground clocks (gravitational + velocity effects). This is corrected in the onboard oscillator — without the correction, GPS would be wrong by ~11 km after one day.

5 · Time distribution — how the world synchronizes

UTC

Universal Coordinated Time — averaged from ~400 atomic clocks worldwide, steered to match Earth rotation via occasional leap seconds.

NTP RFC 5905

Network Time Protocol — syncs computers over the internet to ~10 ms. Used by every OS on Earth.

PTP (IEEE 1588)

Precision Time Protocol — syncs LAN devices to <1 μs. Used in finance (timestamping trades), broadcast, 5G.

GPS disciplining

A local OCXO locked to GPS time gives atomic-clock stability without owning an atomic clock. Used in every cell tower.

Leap second

Earth rotation is irregular — UTC adds/removes a second occasionally. Scheduled to be abolished by 2035. Has caused real outages (Reddit 2012, Cloudflare 2017).

TAI vs UTC

TAI = pure atomic time, no leap seconds. Currently UTC = TAI − 37 s. GPS time = TAI − 19 s. Lots of off-by-N-seconds bugs in software.

Big picture: every digital device is nested inside time references. Your CPU's 5 GHz PLL rides on a 25 MHz crystal which gets disciplined by NTP which traces back to cesium atoms at NIST. The "tick" of a CPU is ultimately a ratio to the vibration of a cesium atom.

🚪 Silicon gates — the atoms of computation

A "gate" is where raw physics becomes logic. Everything above this line (adders, floats, neural nets) is just gates wired together.

🧒 Imagine this…

Think of a garden hose with a valve. Water comes in one end, goes out the other. The valve has a handle. Twist the handle one way — water flows. Twist the other way — water stops. That's a transistor.

The handle has a special name: the gate terminal. The water-in-end is called source. The water-out-end is called drain. In a real transistor, there's no water — there's electricity. But the idea is exactly the same.

Now here's the clever part: you can hook the drain of one transistor straight into the gate handle of the next transistor. So the first transistor's output twists the handle of the second one. Wire 2 transistors together like this and you get a "NAND" — the tiniest possible rule. Wire a billion of them and you get an entire brain made of glass and electricity.

The word "gate" gets used two ways below — one is the handle on a transistor, the other is a small rule-circuit. Both gatekeep something, which is why they share the name.

⚠️ Two meanings of "gate" (both used here)

1. Gate terminal (physics)

One of the 3 wires on a MOSFET transistor: source, drain, gate. The gate terminal is a tiny metal plate sitting above the silicon channel, separated by ~1 nm of insulating oxide. It's the control knob — voltage here creates an electric field that reaches through the insulator into the silicon, pulling electrons into the channel (or not).

2. Logic gate (circuit)

A small circuit of 2-8 transistors that implements a Boolean function: NAND, AND, OR, XOR, NOT. Built by wiring the gate terminals of multiple transistors together. The word "gate" is reused because both act as gatekeepers of signal flow.

The MOSFET — 3 terminals, 1 switch

┌── GATE ──┐ ← control input (voltage here) │ │ SOURCE ────┤ ├──── DRAIN │ │ └──────────┘ ▲ (silicon channel)

Source

Where electrons come from. Usually wired to 0 V (ground) in an n-type MOSFET.

Drain

Where electrons flow to. Wired to the output node — its voltage is the "answer" the transistor produces.

Gate terminal

The control input. HIGH voltage (~0.8 V) → channel conducts → transistor ON (logic 1). LOW voltage (0 V) → channel blocked → transistor OFF (logic 0). The gate itself doesn't touch the channel; it controls it through an electric field.

Garden hose analogy: source = tap, drain = nozzle, gate terminal = the valve handle. Twist the handle, water flows; release it, water stops. The gate terminal is the valve handle of a transistor — and computing is 208 billion of them being twisted on and off, billions of times per second, in perfect coordination.
How gate terminals cascade into logic: the output voltage at the drain of one transistor is wired directly into the gate terminal of the next. That's the whole chain: drain → gate → drain → gate → … → LLM response. Each step is a voltage decision, made in ~10 picoseconds.

From transistor to logic gate

What is a logic gate, literally?

Take 2 MOSFET transistors, wire them so that the output voltage is HIGH only when both inputs are LOW. That's a NAND gate. It's a physical object ~20 nm across on the silicon die. Apply 0.8 V to the inputs, watch the output node charge or discharge within ~10 picoseconds.

Why gates matter

A gate turns continuous voltage (analog, noisy, messy) into discrete 0 / 1 (clean, copyable, composable). This quantization is the single most important trick in all of computing — it's why a signal can pass through a billion stages without degrading.

Universality

NAND alone is Turing-complete. Any boolean function — AND, OR, XOR, a 64-bit adder, a tensor core, an entire CPU — can be built from nothing but NAND gates. Same for NOR. This is why chip design reduces to "how many NAND-equivalents do we need?"

The 7 basic gates

NOT (invert), AND (both 1), OR (either 1), NAND/NOR (negated), XOR (different), XNOR (same). A 1-bit full adder = 2 XORs + 2 ANDs + 1 OR = ~20 transistors. A 64-bit adder ≈ 1,500 transistors.

Gate delay (the speed limit)

Every gate takes time to switch — typically 5–20 picoseconds on modern silicon. A 3 GHz CPU has a 333 ps clock cycle, so ~20–60 gates can fire serially per cycle. This is why pipelining (breaking work into short gate chains) exists.

Gate count ≈ capability

Intel 4004 (1971): ~2,300 transistors ≈ 600 gates — just enough for a 4-bit adder and some registers. B200 (2024): 208 B transistors ≈ 50 B gates — enough for 20,000 tensor cores, 192 GB HBM controllers, and NVLink fabric. Scale is the only real difference.

The abstraction stack you're standing on:
electrons → MOSFET (source/gate/drain) → NAND logic gate → 1-bit adder → 64-bit ALU → CPU core → ISA → compiler → Python → PyTorch → LLM.
Every layer above "NAND gate" is a human invention. Everything below is physics.

🔟 Integers — unsigned, signed, and 2's complement

🧒 Imagine this…

You have 8 coins in a row. Each coin can show heads (1) or tails (0). The first coin is worth 128 points, the next 64, then 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Add up the heads and you get a number between 0 and 255. Flip any coin and watch the number change!

But wait — what about negative numbers? Computers use a clever trick called 2's complement: the very first coin is worth negative 128 instead of +128. So 10000000 means −128, and 11111111 means −1. Sounds weird, but it means the same circuit that does plus also does minus — one less thing to build.

Why 2's complement won

One adder works for both

Signed and unsigned addition use the same hardware. −3 + 5 is just 0xFD + 0x05 = 0x102 → 0x02. The overflow carry out is ignored.

Unique zero

Sign-magnitude and 1's complement both have +0 and −0, doubling equality logic. 2's complement has exactly one zero.

⚠️ The asymmetric range

The smallest 8-bit signed int is −128 but the largest is +127. So −INT_MIN overflows — it's a classic bug in abs() implementations.

⚠️ Signed overflow is UB in C

Compilers assume signed arithmetic never overflows (lets them optimize more aggressively). Use unsigned or __builtin_add_overflow when you care.

How negation works in 2's complement

Two steps: flip all bits, then add 1. That's it. Hardware implements this as NOT → adder.

🔄 Endianness — byte order in memory

Same 32-bit integer, two different memory layouts. Enter a value and watch the bytes rearrange.

🧒 Imagine this…

You have a big number — say, 1,234. You need to put it into 4 little cubbies, one digit per cubby. Which way around do you put it?

Way A (big-endian): biggest first — cubby 1 gets "1", cubby 2 gets "2", cubby 3 gets "3", cubby 4 gets "4". Reads left-to-right like a book.
Way B (little-endian): smallest first — cubby 1 gets "4", cubby 2 gets "3", cubby 3 gets "2", cubby 4 gets "1". Reads backwards.

Both are valid! Both store the same number. But if one chip writes it Way A and another chip reads it Way B, you get gibberish. Intel/AMD chips use Way B. Most network traffic uses Way A. Every time a packet crosses the internet, it gets flipped. This silly disagreement has caused 50 years of bugs.

Big Endian (MSB first)

Used by: network byte order, PowerPC, SPARC, Motorola 68k, JVM bytecode

Little Endian (LSB first)

Used by: x86, x86-64, ARM (default), RISC-V, most modern CPUs

Why big-endian feels natural

We write numbers MSB-first (human convention). Big-endian matches memory-dump reading. It's called "network byte order" because early protocols (TCP, DNS) standardized on it.

Why little-endian won CPUs

Reading the low byte of a wider integer is just a byte load at the same address. Pointer casts (uint32_t* → uint8_t*) give you the low byte for free — useful for arithmetic carry propagation and variable-width data.

Bug magnet: writing a binary file on x86 (LE) and reading it on a big-endian system (or vice versa) without swapping bytes corrupts every multi-byte field. Use htonl/ntohl at protocol boundaries, or use a self-describing format (protobuf, FlatBuffers) that handles endianness for you.

🌊 Floating Point — IEEE 754 everywhere

Scientific notation, in binary, in hardware. Type a number and watch it dissolve into sign/exponent/mantissa.

🧒 Imagine this…

Regular (integer) numbers are fine for counting apples, but what about really tiny things like the size of an atom (0.0000000001 metres) or really big things like the distance to a star (40,000,000,000,000,000 metres)? Writing all those zeros is silly.

Scientists invented a shortcut: "the number × 10 to some power". So the atom is 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ and the star is 4 × 10¹⁶. Much shorter! Computers do the same trick, but in binary: sign × mantissa × 2^exponent.

A float has 3 parts squished into one: sign (+ or −), exponent (how big/small), mantissa (the actual digits). The price you pay: floats can't always be exact. That's why 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 in almost every computer — a famous bug that trips up every programmer eventually.

The classic gotchas

0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3

0.1 is a repeating fraction in binary. The rounding error leaks into addition. 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004 in every IEEE 754 language.

Subnormals are slow

When the exponent would underflow, IEEE uses gradual underflow (subnormals). On some CPUs these trigger 10–100× slower paths. Use -ffast-math or "flush-to-zero" modes if bit-exact doesn't matter.

NaN ≠ NaN

The only value that's not equal to itself. x != x is the fastest portable NaN check. NaNs propagate through arithmetic — one NaN poisons everything downstream.

Catastrophic cancellation

Subtracting near-equal numbers destroys precision. sin(x) − x near 0 loses most bits. Use Taylor expansions or fma (fused multiply-add) to preserve intermediate precision.

📐 IEEE 754 — the scientific notation, decoded

The §Floats section showed what a float looks like. This section shows how to read the bits — the exact formula, the bias trick, the 5 kinds of values, and worked examples you can do by hand.

🧒 Imagine this…

Remember scientific notation from school? 6.022 × 10²³ for Avogadro's number. It has three parts: a sign (+), a mantissa (6.022), and an exponent (23). IEEE 754 is exactly that idea, but in binary and squished into a fixed number of bits.

The clever bit: engineers in 1985 wrote down one universal recipe — every CPU and GPU on Earth now uses the same bit layout. So a float written by a calculator in 1987, an iPhone in 2024, and a GPU training GPT-5 all mean exactly the same thing.

Once you learn to read the bits, a float stops being magic. It's just three tiny boxes (sign, exponent, mantissa) with a fixed formula connecting them to a real number.

The master formula

value = (-1)S × (1 + M / 2m) × 2(E − bias) S = sign bit (1 bit) E = biased exponent (e bits, stored as unsigned) M = mantissa/fraction (m bits, the "1." is implicit) bias = 2(e−1) − 1 (127 for FP32, 1023 for FP64)

Why bias? storage trick

Exponents need to be negative (for very small numbers) and positive (for very large). But storing signed ints needs two's complement — which breaks sorting floats as ints.

Fix: store E_stored = E_true + bias, always as unsigned. For FP32, real exponent −127…+128 → stored 0…255.

Bonus: floats compare correctly with integer compare when both positive. Key to GPU sorting kernels.

Why implicit leading 1? free bit

In binary scientific notation, the mantissa is always 1.xxxxx (normalized). So the leading 1 is never stored — you get it for free.

FP32 stores 23 mantissa bits but actually has 24 bits of precision. Same trick in every IEEE format except subnormals.

"The hidden bit" — one of the cleverest encoding tricks in computer history.

Worked example: decoding 3.14 as FP32

(-1)⁰ × 1.10010001111010111000011₂ × 2¹ = 3.1400001049041748... ← not exactly 3.14

Signed zero? +0 vs −0

Yes, +0 == −0 compares true, but their bit patterns differ. Matters for 1/+0 = +∞ vs 1/−0 = −∞, and for complex-function branch cuts.

Subnormals gradual underflow

Without subnormals, there'd be a gap around 0 where a − b could underflow abruptly. Subnormals fill that gap linearly — gradual precision loss, not a cliff. Cost: often slow in hardware (FTZ/DAZ flags disable them).

Quiet vs signalling NaN qNaN / sNaN

qNaN propagates silently (MSB of mantissa = 1). sNaN raises a CPU exception (MSB = 0). Most languages only use qNaN. The 22 remaining mantissa bits are a payload — used for diagnostic codes.

Rounding modes 5 options

IEEE 754 mandates 5: round-to-nearest-even (default), round-toward-0 (truncate), round-toward-+∞, round-toward-−∞, round-to-nearest-away. Financial code often prefers away-from-zero; statistical code uses banker's rounding (nearest-even).

History & why it matters

William Kahan Turing Award 1989

Berkeley professor, architect of IEEE 754. Before 754, every CPU had its own float format — a Cray float meant nothing on a VAX. Kahan pushed for a universal standard with reproducible results.

Drafted 1985 · revised 2008, 2019

8087 coprocessor Intel, 1980

First hardware implementation of draft IEEE 754. Plug-in chip for the 8086. Before it, floats were done in software — 100× slower. Every CPU since has built-in float.

Why it's a miracle portability

Every phone, laptop, GPU, and supercomputer on Earth agrees on the exact bit-for-bit meaning of every float. That's not true of most of computing. Endianness varies, integer sizes vary, char sets vary — but 0x40490FDB is π on all of them.

The exceptions holdouts

NVIDIA's TF32 (19 bits, non-standard), AMD's FP24, Google TPU's bfloat16 (now standardized), Posit/Unum (John Gustafson's rival). All extensions or alternatives, none has displaced IEEE 754.

🪄 Fractions — the 4 ways computers fake them

Floats are one answer to "how do I store ½?" — but not the only one. Here are all four approaches and when each one wins.

🧒 Imagine this…

Counting whole apples is easy: 1, 2, 3. But what about half an apple? Or a third? Computers only have on/off switches, so how do they store "0.5" or "1/3"?

Turns out there's a deep truth: computers can't store most fractions perfectly. They always have to fudge a bit. Different ways of fudging work best for different jobs. Scientists, bankers, game engines, and AI chips each pick a different method.

The biggest shock: typing 0.1 + 0.2 in almost any programming language gives you 0.30000000000000004, not 0.3. That's not a bug — it's physics. Keep reading to see why.

The deep problem

There are infinitely many real numbers, but only finitely many bit patterns. With 32 bits you can represent at most 2³² = ~4.3 billion distinct values. But between 0 and 1 alone there are uncountably many real numbers. So most fractions cannot be stored exactly, ever. Every scheme below is a different compromise about which fractions it gets exactly right and how it handles the rest.

The 4 approaches

1 · Floating-point default · binary

Already covered in §Floats. sign · mantissa · 2^exponent in binary. Fast (hardware), huge range, but most decimals aren't exact in base 2.

0.1 binary = 0.0001100110011... (repeats forever) → gets chopped at 23 or 52 bits

Exact only when: denominator is a power of 2 (½, ¼, ⅛, ⅜, …). Everything else is rounded.

C float/double, Python float, GPU mathWikipedia ↗

2 · Fixed-point classic · exact

Use a regular integer but pretend the decimal point lives at a fixed position. So 12345 with 2-decimal scaling means 123.45. Do integer math, interpret the result.

Format "16.16" → 32-bit int · top 16 bits = whole part · bottom 16 = fraction

Perfectly exact within its range. No rounding surprises. Fast — just integer arithmetic.

Downside: no auto-scaling. Overflow becomes your problem.

Banking · PS1 games · DSPs · blockchain (wei = 10⁻¹⁸ ETH)Wikipedia ↗

3 · Rational (p / q) symbolic · exact

Store numerator and denominator as separate integers. 1/3 is literally the pair (1, 3). Add, multiply, simplify.

Python: Fraction(1,3) + Fraction(1,3) + Fraction(1,3) == 1 exactly

Perfectly exact for any rational. Bignum means arbitrary precision.

Downside: slow (GCD on every op), memory grows, no hardware support, still can't do √2 or π.

Mathematica · SymPy · Lean · proof assistants · CASWikipedia ↗

4 · Decimal float financial grade

Like FP32/FP64 but in base 10. So 0.1 is exactly 0.1, not an approximation. Standardized as IEEE 754-2008 (decimal32/64/128).

Python Decimal('0.1') + Decimal('0.2') == Decimal('0.3') ✓

IBM mainframes have hardware decimal-float — POWER, z/Architecture. That's how credit-card processors do billions of transactions with cent-exact rounding.

Java BigDecimal · C# decimal · Python decimal · SQL NUMERICWikipedia ↗

The infamous "0.1 + 0.2" bug

Try in Python / JavaScript / C / anywhere with FP: >>> 0.1 + 0.2 0.30000000000000004 # not 0.3! Why? 0.1 in binary never terminates: 0.1₁₀ = 0.0001100110011001100110011...₂ ← repeats forever FP64 must truncate after 52 mantissa bits: stored as 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827021181583404541015625 Same for 0.2. Two small errors. Add them → error doesn't cancel.

⚙️ Assembly — how silicon sees arithmetic

Every a + b becomes 1–4 bytes of machine code. Here's what actually runs.

🧒 Imagine this…

Computers are really really dumb. They don't know "add these two numbers". They only know super tiny baby-steps, like: "pick up the number from box 3", "pick up the number from box 7", "add them", "put the answer in box 12". Each baby-step has a 3-letter nickname — things like MOV (move) and ADD.

That list of nicknames is called assembly language. It's the simplest language a CPU understands. Every app on your phone, every game, every website — when you boil it all down — is just millions of these little 3-letter baby-steps lined up in order.

Different chips understand different nicknames: Intel/AMD speak x86, Apple chips and phones speak ARM, newcomers speak RISC-V. They're like different languages for giving the same instructions.

x86-64 — add two integers

// C: int c = a + b;
mov     eax, [rbp-4]   ; load a
add     eax, [rbp-8]   ; eax += b
mov     [rbp-12], eax  ; store c
3 instructions, ~3 clock cycles best case.

ARM64 — the same thing

// C: int c = a + b;
ldr     w0, [sp, #12]   ; load a
ldr     w1, [sp, #8]    ; load b
add     w0, w0, w1        ; w0 = a + b
str     w0, [sp, #4]    ; store c
Fixed-length 4-byte instructions · load-store RISC.

🛠️ Compilers — the translation layer

A compiler turns x = a + b * c into an instruction stream that runs fast on this specific CPU.

🧒 Imagine this…

You speak English. The computer only speaks in tiny 3-letter baby-steps (assembly). Who translates? A compiler. It's a special program whose whole job is: read your code, turn it into thousands of baby-steps, and make those baby-steps as fast as possible.

Translating isn't enough — the compiler also rearranges and removes steps to save time. If you write x = 2 + 3, a smart compiler just writes down x = 5 and skips the whole add. If you write the same thing twice, it might only do it once. If your computer has a special speed-shortcut, the compiler will secretly use it.

A well-written compiler can make your program run 100× faster than a naïve one. Famous compilers: GCC, Clang/LLVM (what Apple uses), MSVC (Windows). Without them, modern computing wouldn't exist — nobody would write 100 million baby-steps by hand.

The three stages

Frontend

Parse source → AST → check types. Language-specific (clang for C/C++, rustc for Rust). Produces LLVM IR (a portable virtual assembly).

Middle-end

IR-level optimizations — dozens of passes. Works on any target. This is where the magic happens.

Backend

IR → target assembly. Register allocation, instruction scheduling, SIMD codegen. Target-specific (x86, ARM, GPU PTX).

🏗️ LLVM & GCC — the two compilers that run the world

Almost every piece of software you've ever run was built by one of these two toolchains. Here's how they're organized, how they differ, and why LLVM quietly took over.

🧒 Imagine this…

Imagine you want to translate a storybook from English into 30 different languages, on 30 different kinds of typewriter. Doing it the naïve way means writing 30 × 30 = 900 separate translators. Exhausting.

Smart idea: invent a secret in-between language. Translate English → secret-language once. Then translate secret-language → each typewriter. Now you only need 30 + 30 = 60 translators. That secret in-between language is called IR (intermediate representation), and it's the whole trick behind modern compilers.

GCC (1987) and LLVM (2003) both use this trick, but LLVM built its IR to be easy for other tools to read. That's why Apple's Swift, Rust, Julia, Zig, and even parts of CUDA are all powered by LLVM today — they reuse its back-end and didn't have to reinvent it.

The universal 3-stage shape

Any modern compiler = 3 boxes + 1 shared language in the middle: ┌─────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ │ FRONT-END │ → │ OPTIMIZER │ → │ BACK-END │ │ (language) │ │ (middle) │ │ (machine) │ └─────────────┘ └──────────────┘ └─────────────┘ C, C++, IR (intermediate x86, ARM, Rust, Swift, representation) RISC-V, Fortran… — the lingua franca WebAssembly, GPU PTX… Parse → AST → IR → ~100 optimization passes → IR → instruction selection → register allocation → binary

The two giants

GCC · The GNU Compiler Collection 1987 · RMS

GCC — written by Richard Stallman as the cornerstone of GNU. Built Linux, BSD, most of Unix. Monolithic by design (political: prevent proprietary front-ends). Compiles C, C++, Fortran, Go, Ada, D, Objective-C. GPLv3 licensed.

IR: GIMPLE (high-level) and RTL (low-level, register-transfer). Internal, rarely touched by outsiders.

Strengths: unmatched target coverage (40+ ISAs: AVR, MSP430, SPARC, m68k, s390x, …), battle-tested since 1987, Fortran + embedded Linux ecosystems, often slightly better runtime perf on x86 integer code.

🤖 The AI era — precision as a knob

Deep learning discovered that neural nets don't need 32-bit precision. That discovery rewrote the entire arithmetic stack.

🧒 Imagine this…

For 50 years, computers used super precise numbers — like 3.14159265358979 — because scientists needed exact answers for rockets and bridges. Each number took 32 little switches to store.

Then AI came along and discovered something magical: for teaching a computer to recognize cats, you don't need 3.14159265358979 — you can just use "about 3" and it works just as well! And "about 3" only needs 4 switches instead of 32. That's 8× less work.

So AI chips keep shrinking their numbers: first FP32 (32 switches) → then FP16 → FP8 → now FP4 (just 4 switches!). The very newest model, BitNet, uses only 1.58 switches per number on average. Each shrink makes AI faster, cheaper, and smaller — without making it dumber. This is the single biggest reason modern AI became affordable.

Why lower precision works

Gradients are noisy anyway

Stochastic gradient descent adds random-batch noise ~10⁻³. FP16's ~10⁻³ precision is below that floor — so FP16 training converges identically to FP32, for free.

Weights are over-parameterized

A typical LLM has 10⁹–10¹² parameters for tasks that need ~10⁸ bits of information. Quantizing 32-bit weights to 4-bit recovers accuracy within 1% on most benchmarks.

Memory bandwidth dominates

On modern GPUs, math is free and memory is expensive. Halving precision halves the bytes moved → nearly doubles throughput on memory-bound layers (attention, MLP).

Tensor cores are specialized

A modern GPU has FP32 ALUs for general math AND tensor cores that ONLY do matrix multiply-accumulate in mixed precision. 20–100× throughput for the right workload.

The precision ladder — FP32 → FP4 explained

Every format below is sign / exponent / mantissa squished into fewer bits. Fewer bits = faster, cheaper, more fits in memory — but less accurate and narrower range. The art is matching the format to the workload.

FP32 32 bits · IEEE 754 single

S│EEEEEEEE│MMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMMM
1 · 8 bits · 23 bits mantissa

Range: ±10³⁸ · Precision: ~7 decimal digits.

The scientific computing default since 1985. Used for graphics, physics, CAD, almost all classical ML before 2017. Every CPU and GPU has native FP32 math.

3.14 → 0x40490FDB (exact stored value 3.1400001049…)

FP16 16 bits · IEEE half

S│EEEEE│MMMMMMMMMM
1 · 5 bits · 10 bits mantissa

Range: ±65,504 (tiny!) · Precision: ~3-4 digits.

Problem: exponent only 5 bits — gradients in deep nets frequently overflow or underflow. "Loss scaling" tricks (multiply loss by 2¹⁵ before backprop, divide after) are needed. Painful.

Introduced by NVIDIA Pascal (2016). Made FP16 training possible but fragile.

BF16 · "Brain Float" 16 bits · Google 2018

S│EEEEEEEE│MMMMMMM
1 · 8 bits · 7 bits mantissa

Range: ±10³⁸ (same as FP32!) · Precision: ~2-3 digits.

The insight: keep FP32's 8-bit exponent (wide range), sacrifice mantissa instead. Neural nets care about range, not precision. No loss scaling needed. Drop-in replacement for FP32 in training.

Invented for Google TPU. Now standard on A100, H100, MI300X, TPU v4+, everything.

TF32 19 bits used · NVIDIA 2020

S│EEEEEEEE│MMMMMMMMMM│xxxxxxxxxxxxx
1 · 8 bits · 10 bits · 13 bits ignored

Not a storage format — data is stored as FP32, but tensor cores read only the top 19 bits during matmul. Invisible speed boost.

Ampere (A100) default for torch.matmul. ~10× faster than true FP32, no code changes. Loses 13 mantissa bits during matmul only.

Why? Mantissa bits in matmul are mostly noise after accumulation — 10 is plenty.

FP8 E4M3 8 bits · weights/acts

S│EEEE│MMM
1 · 4 bits · 3 bits mantissa

Range: ±448 · Precision: ~1-2 digits. Only 256 distinct values total.

Used for forward pass: weights and activations. Needs per-tensor or per-block scaling factors (dynamic scaling kept separately) to make the tiny range usable across layers.

Hopper H100 Transformer Engine auto-manages scales layer-by-layer.

FP8 E5M2 8 bits · gradients

S│EEEEE│MM
1 · 5 bits · 2 bits mantissa

Range: ±57,344 · Precision: ~1 digit.

Used for backward pass: gradients have much wider dynamic range (often 10⁻⁶ to 10³) so we trade mantissa for exponent. Paired with E4M3 in mixed-precision training.

Two FP8 formats, one workload — that's the trick: use E4M3 where range is tight, E5M2 where range is wide.

FP4 E2M1 4 bits · Blackwell 2024

S│EE│M
1 · 2 bits · 1 bit mantissa

Range: ±6 · Only 16 distinct values total.

Ships on NVIDIA Blackwell B200 (2024). Delivers 20 PFLOPS FP4 per chip — 2× FP8, 4× BF16. Lets you fit a 70B-parameter model in 35 GB instead of 140 GB. Inference only (no training).

MXFP4 variant: 32 FP4 values share one 8-bit exponent scale → "microscaled" for accuracy.

INT8 / INT4 integer quantization

INT8: -128…127 · INT4: -8…7
No sign/exp/mantissa — just plain integers + a scale

Store weights as integers with a per-tensor (or per-row) FP16 scale: real = int × scale. Used for post-training quantization with tools like GPTQ, AWQ, bitsandbytes.

Llama-70B INT4 fits in 40 GB of VRAM on a single consumer GPU.

BitNet b1.58 1.58 bits · Microsoft 2024

Each weight ∈ {−1, 0, +1}
log₂(3) ≈ 1.58 bits per weight

No multiplies needed — matrix multiply becomes add/subtract/skip. 10× less energy than FP16 LLMs of equal quality. Suggests the precision floor is far below what current hardware supports.

If true at scale, future AI chips need only adders — no multipliers.

Why not always use the smallest?

Overflow range too small

FP16 max is 65,504. One layer of a ResNet can easily output 10⁵ — boom, all infs. That's why BF16 (FP32 range) won over FP16.

Underflow gradients vanish

Gradients near the input of a deep net are tiny (10⁻⁸). FP16 flushes them to zero. Training stalls. Loss scaling is the patch.

Accuracy collapse information loss

FP4 has only 16 values. A careless conversion from FP16 drops ~3% top-1 on ImageNet. Requires calibration (GPTQ, SmoothQuant) or QAT to recover.

Outliers attention is spiky

Transformer attention has a few activations that are 100× larger than the rest. Naïve quantization clips them, ruining the model. Solutions: per-channel scales, outlier-aware quant (SmoothQuant, AWQ).

Accumulation must stay wide

Inputs can be FP8, but the accumulator inside the multiply-add must stay FP32 (or at least BF16). Otherwise tiny errors compound across thousands of multiplies in a single matmul.

Calibration cost data needed

Lower-bit formats need a small calibration dataset to pick scales. That's extra infrastructure. Simpler to train in BF16 and ship the model as-is.

Rule of thumb used in practice:
  • 📐 Train in BF16 with FP32 master weights + FP32 accumulator. (Or FP8 with Transformer Engine on H100+.)
  • ⚡ Serve in FP8 or INT8 for latency; INT4 / FP4 for throughput on memory-bound LLMs.
  • 💰 Edge / mobile: INT8 (widely supported on ARM, Apple Neural Engine, Qualcomm Hexagon).
  • 🔬 Research: BitNet b1.58, MXFP4, microscaling — the frontier is 1-2 bits per weight.

Quantization — shrinking models after training

Post-training quantization (PTQ)

Train in FP16/BF16, then cast weights to INT8 or INT4 using calibration data to pick scales. Works out of the box for convnets; needs care for transformers (attention is sensitive).

Tools: GPTQ, AWQ, bitsandbytes, TensorRT.

Quantization-aware training (QAT)

Simulate low precision during training so the model learns to be robust. Recovers most of the quality loss of aggressive quantization at the cost of 2× training time.

Gives ~2× better accuracy than PTQ at INT4.

BitNet b1.58 — the 1.58-bit LLM

Each weight is one of {−1, 0, +1}, encoding in log₂(3) ≈ 1.58 bits. Matches FP16 LLMs of the same size, uses 10× less energy. Suggests the precision floor is far below what current hardware supports.

Blackwell FP4 (2024)

NVIDIA B200 runs 4-bit floats natively: 20 PFLOPS FP4. Ideal for LLM inference — a 70B model fits in 35 GB instead of 140 GB.

Tensor cores, systolic arrays & TPUs

A GPU core can do 1 FP32 multiply-add per cycle. A tensor core does a 4×4×4 matrix multiply (64 multiplies + 48 adds) in a single instruction. TPUs generalize this: Google's TPU v5p has a 256×256 systolic array — 65536 multiply-accumulators on one chip, moving data in rhythm without register file pressure.

Insight: the AI revolution didn't invent new math. It discovered that classical linear algebra — matrix multiply, softmax, Gauss's normal equations — runs orders of magnitude faster when you let precision be a tunable parameter and when memory layout matches the kernel (coalesced, tiled, streamed).

📏 "208 billion transistors" — what that actually means

Let's build the number up from scratch. No jargon, no shortcuts.

🧒 Imagine this…

Imagine a light switch so tiny you could fit 5,000 of them across the width of a single human hair. Now imagine you packed 208 billion of them onto a piece of silicon the size of a postage stamp, and they all flip on and off 3 billion times every second — in perfect coordination.

That's not a metaphor. That's literally what's inside the chip that trains ChatGPT. Each tiny switch is called a transistor, and counting them is how we measure how "smart" a chip can be.

Below we'll build up what 208 billion really means — step by step, no shortcuts.

Step 1 · Start with one transistor

How big is it?

A single transistor on a modern chip is about 20 nanometres wide. That's 20 billionths of a metre — roughly 20 silicon atoms across. You could line up 5,000 of them inside the width of a single human hair.

What does it do?

It's a switch. Turn the voltage on its gate terminal ON → electricity flows (that's a 1). Turn it OFF → no electricity (that's a 0). That's it. That's the whole thing. Every computer does only this, just many times over.

How fast?

It flips on/off about 3 billion times a second (~3 GHz). Each flip takes maybe 10 picoseconds — in that time, light travels only 3 millimetres.

Step 2 · Now picture 208 billion of them

208,000,000,000

Two hundred and eight billion. If you started counting at 1 per second, it would take you 6,600 years to finish. If each transistor were 1 mm wide, laid end-to-end they'd wrap around the Earth 5 times.

All in your palm

Every one of those 208 billion switches fits on a piece of silicon about the size of a postage stamp (~1,600 mm²). The whole B200 chip package is smaller than a playing card.

Actually two chips

208 B was too many to fit on one piece of silicon — NVIDIA ran into a physical limit called the reticle limit (the biggest rectangle their lithography machine can print, ~858 mm²). So they made two identical chips of 104 B each and bonded them together with a high-speed link. The software thinks it's one chip.

Sidebar · What is "4 nm process"?

You'll see phrases like "built on TSMC's 4 nm process". Here's what that means.

The number = how small the features are

"Process" (or "process node", "fab node") is the manufacturing technology used to print the chip. The number is roughly the smallest feature the factory can etch into silicon. Smaller = more transistors in the same area = faster, cooler chips.

Putting the sizes in context

10 µm (1971): thicker than a red blood cell.
180 nm (2000): about the size of a virus.
28 nm (2013): width of a DNA strand.
4 nm (2024): ~20 silicon atoms wide.
2 nm (2025+): ~10 atoms — bumping into quantum limits.

⚠️ It's partly marketing now

Originally "90 nm" literally meant the smallest wire was 90 nm wide. Since ~2010, the number is mostly branding. TSMC's "4 nm" doesn't actually have any 4 nm feature — real smallest dimensions are more like 20–30 nm. The name tracks relative improvement across generations, not literal size.

What each shrink buys you

Each "node shrink" roughly: 2× more transistors in the same area · ~30% faster switching · ~30% less power per operation. Stack 25 shrinks on top of each other and you get: a 2024 phone outperforming a 2000 supercomputer.

Why it's slowing down

Below ~5 nm, transistors are so small that electrons quantum-tunnel through the gate insulator (they leak even when "off"). So the industry keeps inventing new 3D transistor shapes: flat → FinFET (2011) → GAAFET (2022). Each new shape buys a few more nodes before physics catches up.

The players

TSMC (Taiwan) — makes chips for Apple, NVIDIA, AMD. On 3 nm, ramping 2 nm.
Samsung — competing 3 nm, different transistor shape.
Intel — renamed nodes (Intel 7 / 4 / 3 / 20A) to keep up in the marketing race.

Step 4 · What those 208 billion switches actually do

Some are memory

A lot of the transistors (~half) just remember numbers — they form on-chip caches that hold the numbers the chip is working on. Bringing numbers from far-away RAM is 100× slower, so the chip stores copies nearby.

Some do math

The other half form multiplier circuits. A 16-bit float multiplier takes roughly 2,000 transistors. B200 has ~20,000 of these multiplier units, all running at once, all finishing one multiplication every clock tick. That's where "20 quadrillion operations per second" comes from.

Some just connect things

The rest is wiring — billions of microscopic traces that route bits between memory, multipliers, and the outside world. The wires alone stack in ~15 layers above the transistors, adding up to tens of kilometres of wire per chip.

The one-line summary: 208 billion transistors = about 100 billion tiny switches that remember numbers + 100 billion that multiply numbers, all on a postage-stamp-sized chip, all flipping 3 billion times a second. That's the whole trick behind modern AI.
Why this is unprecedented: transistor count on a single chip has roughly doubled every 2 years for 50 years straight (Moore's Law). 4004 → B200 is a 90-million-fold jump. Nothing else humans build scales like this — not cars, not buildings, not engines. Only silicon.

⚡ FLOPS, PFLOPS, EFLOPS — reading the speed numbers

Every AI press release quotes these. Here's how to decode them.

🧒 Imagine this…

Grown-ups always say things like "this chip does 20 PFLOPS!" What even is that?

A FLOP is one math problem with decimal numbers — like 3.14 × 2.71. That's one FLOP. FLOPS (with an S, but the S means "per second", NOT plural!) means how many math problems the chip solves every single second.

A letter in front tells you how many: Kilo = thousand. Mega = million. Giga = billion. Tera = trillion. Peta = quadrillion. Exa = quintillion. So "20 PFLOPS" = 20 quadrillion math problems every second. If every human on Earth did one multiplication per second, the whole planet could only do 8 billion per second. One AI chip = 2.5 million Earths.

One FLOP

= one floating-point operation. A single add, subtract, multiply, or divide on numbers like 3.14 × 2.71. An FMA (fused multiply-add) counts as 2 FLOPs by convention — it's the workhorse of both graphics and AI.

FLOPS (with the S)

= FLOPs per second. The "S" is seconds, not plural. So "20 PFLOPS" means 20 × 10¹⁵ float ops every single second. Rate, not a count.

Precision matters!

A chip's FLOPS number depends on how wide the float is. B200 delivers ~40 TFLOPS at FP64, ~2.2 PFLOPS at FP16, and ~20 PFLOPS at FP4. Smaller floats → more multipliers fit in the same silicon → bigger number. Always ask "at what precision?".

📜 Laws & Limits — the rules that govern computing

Every technology this dashboard describes is bounded by a handful of named laws — some from physics, some from engineering, some from economics. Here they are, with the formulas, the stories, and where they currently stand.

🧒 Imagine this…

When grown-ups design computers, they don't get to decide how fast or how small things can be — the universe decides for them. These "decisions" are called laws. Some come from physics (the universe really won't let you go below them), some from clever observation (they held for 50 years and might keep holding), and some from economics (doubling power costs 4× the money).

Knowing these laws is like knowing the rules of a game. Once you know them, you stop being surprised when your 3 GHz CPU won't become a 30 GHz CPU next year. The universe said no.

Deep dives

Moore's Law 1965 · the defining curve of modernity

"The number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles approximately every two years." — the most successful quantitative prediction in the history of industry. For 60 years it has guided R&D budgets, pricing, Wall Street expectations, and national strategy.

📜 The original paper

In April 1965, Gordon Moore (then research director at Fairchild Semiconductor, 3 years before co-founding Intel) wrote a 4-page article titled "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits" for Electronics Magazine's 35th-anniversary issue.

He plotted 5 data points (1959-1965) on a log scale, noticed components per chip had doubled every year, and predicted it would continue for "at least 10 years" — reaching 65,000 components per chip by 1975. Nearly everyone thought he was crazy. He was off by only ~2×.

"Integrated circuits will lead to such wonders as home computers… automatic controls for automobiles… personal portable communications equipment." — Moore, 1965. He was writing this in a world where a computer filled a room.
🔄 The 1975 revision

By 1975, doubling had slipped to every ~18 months, then ~2 years. Moore officially revised the prediction to "every two years" in a 1975 IEDM speech. Intel co-founder David House later coined "every 18 months for performance" — which added frequency gains to transistor count. All three numbers are still quoted, and all three are called "Moore's Law" in the wild.

⚙️ How it was sustained (the 5 eras)
  1. Bipolar TTL era (1965-1975) — photolithography from visible light, mask-align by hand.
  2. CMOS planar era (1975-2003) — Dennard scaling did the heavy lifting. Every 2 years: dimensions ×0.7, voltage ×0.7, speed ×1.4, density ×2.
  3. Strained silicon / HKMG era (2003-2011) — 90 nm onward. Added strained silicon (faster electrons), high-k/metal-gate (Intel 45 nm, 2007).
  4. FinFET era (2011-2022) — gate wraps 3 sides of channel (Intel 22 nm, 2011; TSMC 16 nm, 2014). Fights leakage; sustains scaling.
  5. GAAFET / nanosheet era (2022+) — gate wraps all 4 sides (Samsung 3 nm GAA, 2022; TSMC N2, 2025; Intel 20A / 18A with RibbonFET). Also 3D stacking (logic-on-logic), backside power, EUV double/triple patterning.

Each era involved a whole-industry reinvention — materials, photolithography (deep-UV → EUV in 2019), transistor architecture, fab tooling. Moore's Law is the result of dozens of different technologies handing the baton.

💀 The three "deaths" of Moore's Law

1. Frequency scaling — died 2004-2006

Pentium 4 "Tejas" cancelled at 3.8 GHz. Clocks stopped climbing. Moore's Law continued on transistor count, but the "your code gets faster for free" era ended. Industry pivoted to multicore.

2. Economic Moore — wobbled ~2014

Until ~28 nm, each new node made transistors cheaper. After 28 nm, fab costs rose faster than density gains. Cost per transistor stopped falling. Moore's Law is still alive physically but not economically — you need AI-scale volume to justify a 3 nm tape-out.

3. Physical Moore — struggling below ~2 nm

At 1-2 nm gate length, quantum tunneling (see §Physics card 1) dominates and conventional CMOS approaches its floor. 2030s innovations (2D materials like MoS₂, carbon nanotubes, 3D stacking) may extend it — but classical Moore's Law probably ends around 2035-2040.

🔭 What "Moore's Law" really tracks (in 2025)

Because frequency died (2005) and cost-per-transistor stalled (~2014), the industry has broadened the definition to keep the curve alive:

  • Transistor count per chip — still doubling every ~2 years via 3D stacking, chiplets, dual-die.
  • Performance per watt — Koomey's Law, still holding at ~2.6-yr doubling.
  • Performance per dollar of AI compute — Huang's Law, doubling faster than Moore due to architecture + precision cuts.
  • Effective density — TSMC now reports "transistors per mm²" instead of just gate length, because "3 nm" is marketing.
🌍 Why it matters — the cultural weight

Moore's Law is not a law of physics. It's a self-fulfilling industry coordination mechanism. Intel told customers to expect 2× every 2 years; customers planned software around it; equipment makers planned roadmaps around it; investors priced stocks around it. Everyone ran their part of the race assuming everyone else would — and because everyone did, it held.

When Moore wrote his 1965 paper, he meant to describe a trend he'd seen for 6 years. It became an economic contract with the future. That's why "the death of Moore's Law" keeps being announced every few years, and yet transistor counts keep doubling. The industry refuses to let it die.

"I never said the doubling was going to happen forever. I just said, in 1965, 'this is going to happen for at least the next 10 years.'" — Gordon Moore, 2005. He lived to see it last 60.

Dennard Scaling 1974 · Robert Dennard

Robert Dennard (IBM, inventor of DRAM) proved: shrink a MOSFET's linear dimensions by factor k → voltage shrinks by k → transistor delay shrinks by k → power per gate shrinks by k² → power density stays constant. That's the miracle that let clocks rise from MHz to GHz.

Why it broke (~2006): below ~90 nm, threshold voltage stopped dropping because subthreshold leakage explodes. V_DD froze at ~1 V. Without falling V, shrinking gates raised power density — chips started melting.

Intel Pentium 4 "Tejas" was designed for 10 GHz, cancelled 2004 at 3.8 GHz because it would have drawn 300 W. The pivot to multicore began right after.

Consequence: the end of Dennard killed frequency scaling. We've been stuck at 3-6 GHz ever since, winning performance through parallelism (multicore, SIMD, GPUs), specialization (tensor cores), and precision cuts (FP16/FP8/FP4).

Physical law while it held · dead since 2006Wikipedia ↗

Koomey's Law 2011 · energy efficiency

Jonathan Koomey (Stanford) — "computations per joule" doubles every ~1.57 years (1950-2000), slowed to ~2.6 years after Dennard broke. Closely related to Moore's Law but orthogonal: Moore is about count, Koomey is about efficiency.

Why it matters: Koomey's Law is what makes battery-powered computing possible. The same work that took 1 kWh in 1985 takes < 1 mJ in 2025. This is why your phone runs AI models that crashed supercomputers a decade ago.

ENIAC (1946): ~500 FLOPS per 150 kW = 3 μFLOPS/W · M2 MacBook (2023): ~3 TFLOPS per 20 W = 150 GFLOPS/W — a 5 × 10¹⁶× efficiency gain.
Tracks compute efficiency · decoupled from clock speedWikipedia ↗

Landauer's Principle 1961 · thermodynamic floor

Rolf Landauer (IBM) proved that erasing 1 bit of information in the presence of a heat bath must dissipate at least kT ln 2 joules — about 2.85 × 10⁻²¹ J at room temperature. Reversible computation has no such limit.

Formula: Emin = kB · T · ln 2

Today we use ~10⁻¹⁴ J per bit operation — about 10⁷× above Landauer. So hardware has ~7 orders of magnitude left before physics stops us. That's 20-30 more Moore doublings, in principle.

Experimental confirmation: Bérut et al., Nature 2012 — measured heat dissipated during a bit-erase on a single colloidal particle, matched kT ln 2 within experimental error.
Physical law · applies to any computation, anywhereWikipedia ↗

Shannon-Hartley Theorem 1948 · channel capacity

Claude Shannon's foundational theorem of information theory. The maximum error-free data rate over a noisy channel is:

C = B · log₂(1 + S/N)

where B is bandwidth (Hz), S is signal power, N is noise power. Doubling bandwidth doubles capacity; doubling signal power adds only 1 bit per symbol. That's why going from 2-level to 4-level signaling (PAM-4) needs 4× the SNR to cover the same error rate.

Governs Wi-Fi, 5G, PCIe 6.0 PAM-4, NAND flash TLC/QLC, and the "why ternary is worse" argument in §Physics.
Information-theoretic ceiling · 77 years old, still untouchableWikipedia ↗

Amdahl's Law 1967 · parallelism ceiling

Gene Amdahl (IBM) — if fraction s of a program is serial and p = 1 − s is parallel, then speedup with N cores is bounded by:

Speedup ≤ 1 / (s + p/N)

Even with infinite cores, max speedup is 1/s. If 5% of your code is serial, you can never exceed 20× — no matter if you have 100 or 1 million cores.

Example: 95% parallel, 5% serial, 16 cores → speedup ≈ 9.1×, not 16×. With 1024 cores → still only 19.8×.

Consequence: multicore-era performance comes from removing serial bottlenecks, not just adding cores. It's why lock-free data structures, SIMD, and cache-coherence protocols matter.

Mathematical ceiling on parallel speedupWikipedia ↗

Gustafson's Law 1988 · optimistic twin

John Gustafson's counterpoint: in practice, people don't run the same-sized problem on more cores — they run bigger problems. If workload scales with cores, speedup is:

Speedup = s + N · p

Linear in N, no ceiling. Explains why supercomputers and GPU clusters actually deliver near-linear speedup despite Amdahl: they're solving larger fluid-dynamics / weather / neural-net problems, not the same problem faster.

LLM training scales to 10,000+ GPUs because you just use more tokens and parameters — "strong scaling" (fixed problem) would plateau.
Complements Amdahl · drives HPC & AI scalingWikipedia ↗

Little's Law 1961 · queueing theory

John Little — for any stable system: L = λ · W. Average items in system (L) = arrival rate (λ) × average time in system (W).

Used everywhere: CPU pipelines (instructions in flight = IPC × depth), queues, databases, networks, Starbucks lines. Derives "requests in flight" capacity for SSDs, NICs, memory controllers.

Example: modern NVMe SSD, 100 μs latency, 1 M IOPS → 100 requests must be in flight (queue depth) to saturate it. Less = wasted bandwidth.
Distribution-independent · holds for any queuing systemWikipedia ↗

Pollack's Rule 1999 · single-core economics

Fred Pollack (Intel) observed: single-core performance grows roughly as √(transistor budget). Double the die area spent on one core → only ~40% faster.

Why: diminishing returns on bigger caches, wider issue, more OoO machinery, branch predictors. Each resource helps less than the last.

Consequence: if √2 ≈ 1.4 is all you get from doubling one core, two cores (2×) beat it. That's the architectural justification for the multicore pivot around 2005.

Explains why CPUs stabilized at ~8-16 cores (sweet spot for desktops) and GPUs went to thousands of tiny cores (throughput wins).
Architectural rule of thumb · drove multicore era

Rent's Rule 1960s IBM · I/O scaling

E. F. Rent (IBM) observed that the number of I/O pins on a logic block grows as a power of the number of gates inside:

P = k · Gᵖ, where p ≈ 0.5 - 0.7

Means: as chips get larger, I/O pin count grows slower than internal gate count. That's why modern GPUs have huge internal bandwidth but relatively few external pins (6,912 pins on B200 package is a lot but trivial vs 208 B transistors).

Drives the packaging crisis: HBM stacking, CoWoS, 2.5D interposers — all solve the "not enough pins" Rent's-rule problem.
Explains why memory bandwidth is always the bottleneckWikipedia ↗

Rock's Law (Moore's 2nd) economics

Arthur Rock — the cost of building a leading-edge fab doubles every 4 years. 1981: $100 M. 1995: $1 B. 2020: $10 B. TSMC's Arizona fab (2024): $65 B.

Consequence: only 3 companies left building leading-edge logic (TSMC, Samsung, Intel). Consolidation is not a business failure — it's Rock's Law eating the industry.

One EUV lithography machine (ASML TWINSCAN NXE) costs $200 million. A fab needs 20+ of them.
Economic law · explains chip-industry consolidationWikipedia ↗

Wirth's Law 1995 · bloat

Niklaus Wirth (inventor of Pascal, Modula, Oberon) — "Software is getting slower more rapidly than hardware is becoming faster."

Every generation of hardware improvements is eaten by JavaScript frameworks, Electron apps, abstraction layers, bloated frameworks. A modern chat app uses more RAM than NASA had when they landed on the Moon.

Gates's corollary: "The speed of software halves every 18 months." Slack uses ~1 GB RAM; so does VS Code. Teams uses 1.5 GB.
Tongue-in-cheek but provableWikipedia ↗

Bell's Law 1972 · new computer classes

Gordon Bell — roughly every decade, a new class of computer emerges at ~100× lower price/size, reaching a new user base: mainframe (1960s) → minicomputer (1970s) → PC (1980s) → laptop (1990s) → smartphone (2000s) → smart-watch / AR (2010s) → ?

Prediction: next class ~2025-2030 — likely always-on AI-first wearables or neural-interface devices. The pattern holds.

Empirical pattern · 6 decades of validationWikipedia ↗

Metcalfe's Law 1980 · networks

Robert Metcalfe (Ethernet inventor) — the value of a network scales as n² where n is the number of users. Doubling users quadruples value.

Why: each user can connect to every other user, giving n(n-1)/2 possible connections.

Explains winner-takes-all dynamics: WhatsApp > Signal, not because tech is better, but because network effect compounds.
Contested (some argue n log n) · still drives tech economicsWikipedia ↗

Kryder's Law 2005 · storage

Mark Kryder (Seagate CTO) — HDD areal density doubles every ~13 months (1990s-2000s), slowed to every ~2-3 years after ~2010 (superparamagnetic limit).

HDDs hit 20 TB (2023) via CMR/SMR, pushing to 30-50 TB via HAMR (Heat-Assisted Magnetic Recording) and MAMR. SSDs track their own curve — NAND density doubles via 3D stacking (128 → 232 → 400+ layers).

Slowing · physical limits of magnetic domainsWikipedia ↗

Huang's Law 2018 · GPU era

Jensen Huang (NVIDIA CEO) — GPU performance for AI doubles every ~2 years, faster than Moore's Law. Tesla K80 (2014) → H100 (2022) → B200 (2024) delivered ~1000× raw AI throughput in a decade.

How (when Moore is slowing): architectural gains (tensor cores), precision cuts (FP32 → FP8 → FP4), better memory (HBM), software (CUDA, Transformer Engine). Four levers instead of one.

Pascal P100 (2016, FP32): 9 TFLOPS · Blackwell B200 (2024, FP4): 20,000 TFLOPS — >2000× in 8 years.
Marketing + engineering · beats Moore via architectureWikipedia ↗

Chinchilla scaling 2022 · DeepMind

For LLMs, compute-optimal training uses ~20 tokens per parameter. A 70 B model should see ~1.4 T tokens; a 1 T model should see ~20 T tokens.

Before Chinchilla: models like GPT-3 (175 B params, 300 B tokens) were under-trained — bigger didn't mean better. After: Llama-2 (70 B × 2 T tokens) beat GPT-3 despite being smaller, by being trained longer.

Rule of thumb: if your model is bigger than your data budget allows, train it less and save compute for a smaller model trained more.

The big picture — which laws are winning

🟢 Alive

Moore (slowly), Koomey, Landauer (untouchable floor), Shannon-Hartley, Amdahl, Gustafson, Little, Rent, Huang, Chinchilla.

🟡 Strained

Kryder (slowing), Metcalfe (contested n log n), Pollack (still predictive but architecture differs).

🔴 Dead / broken

Dennard (dead ~2006), Clock-frequency scaling (dead at ~5 GHz), Single-thread free lunch (dead since 2005).

Why these laws matter for this dashboard: everything above (FP4 precision, multicore, HBM, tensor cores, quantum tunneling floor, 5 GHz wall) is downstream of these rules. AI didn't happen because engineers got smarter — it happened because Dennard died, which forced GPUs, which made Huang's Law take over from Moore's, which made ever-shrinking precision formats profitable. Laws first. Engineering responses second.

E · Assembly, ISA & CPU features

Featured: the ISA — the contract that defines a chip family. Full term table below.

ISA — Instruction Set Architecture ★ the contract

The language a CPU speaks. An ISA specifies: (1) which operations exist (ADD, MOV, JMP, …), (2) what registers the CPU has, (3) exact bit patterns for every instruction. Once published, many different chips can implement the same ISA and all run the same software.

ISA vs microarchitecture: ISA = the specification. Microarchitecture = how it's physically built. Intel Raptor Lake and AMD Zen 4 both speak x86-64 but are completely different chips inside. Apple M3 and Qualcomm Snapdragon both speak ARMv8, totally different internals.

Why you can't run an iPhone app on a Windows laptop → different ISAs (ARM vs x86-64)
The most durable abstraction in computingWikipedia ↗

x86 / x86-64 CISC · 1978

Intel & AMD. Complex instructions, variable length (1–15 bytes). ~1,500 instructions total. Modern chips internally convert them to RISC-like micro-ops.

Windows PCs · Linux servers · data centers · gaming
Duopoly (Intel + AMD)Wikipedia ↗

ARM / AArch64 RISC · 1985

Simple fixed-length (4-byte) instructions. Licensed, not open. Lower power-per-watt → won mobile, now winning laptops and servers.

Every smartphone · Apple Silicon (M-series) · AWS Graviton · NVIDIA Grace
Hundreds of licenseesWikipedia ↗

RISC-V RISC · 2010 · open

From UC Berkeley. Completely open-source, no royalties, anyone can design a chip. Modular: start with base 32-bit, add extensions as needed (M, A, F, D, V, …).

SSD controllers · Western Digital · Alibaba · Tenstorrent · rising fast in embedded & AI accelerators
The geopolitical dark horseWikipedia ↗

CISC vs RISC ancient debate

CISC (x86): few complex instructions, "one instruction does a lot." RISC (ARM, RISC-V): many simple instructions, "let the compiler chain them."

Modern reality: the line is blurred. x86 chips convert CISC instructions into internal RISC-like micro-ops. ARM keeps adding complex extensions. The ISA is often a facade over a different internal machine.

No winner — both are everywhereWikipedia ↗

G · Memory hierarchy

Featured: HBM — the memory that makes modern AI possible. Full latency table below.

HBM — High Bandwidth Memory ★ the AI bottleneck

DRAM chips stacked vertically like a skyscraper (8–12 layers), wired with thousands of microscopic holes straight down (TSVs — through-silicon vias), and placed on the same silicon slab as the GPU, ~5 mm away. Uses a 1024-bit wide bus per stack (vs 64 bits in desktop DDR).

Why it exists: a modern GPU with 20,000 cores eats data at ~8,000 GB/s. Regular DDR5 RAM delivers ~50 GB/s — the GPU would starve. HBM solves this with a wider, shorter, taller memory.

Traditional DDR: HBM: ┌─────┐ ┌──────┐ ┌──────────┐ │ CPU │◄64b►│ DRAM │ │ GPU │ └─────┘ └──────┘ ├──────────┤ (far away · narrow) │ ░ HBM ░ │ ← stack of 8–12 │ ░ ░░░ ░ │ DRAM layers, │ ░ ░░░ ░ │ 1024-bit bus, └──────────┘ ~5 mm from GPU
NVIDIA B200 = 8 stacks × HBM3e = 8 TB/s aggregate across 192 GB

Why AI is memory-bound the roofline truth

GPT-4-sized LLM (~1.7T params, 8-bit) = 1.7 TB of weights. Generating one token = reading every weight once. At 8 TB/s HBM → 4–5 tokens/sec ceiling from bandwidth alone. Compute FLOPS don't help if data can't arrive fast enough.

Everyone talks FLOPS, but bandwidth decides real speed

DRAM vs SRAM two cell types

DRAM: 1 transistor + 1 capacitor per bit. Dense & cheap but must refresh every 64 ms. Used for main memory, HBM. SRAM: 6 transistors per bit. Stable, fast, huge — used for CPU caches (L1/L2/L3).

Same chip might have ~100 MB SRAM cache + 192 GB HBM + 1 TB DDR DRAM
